When a floating body interacts with waves, the forces acting on it depend not only on the incoming wave field, but also on how the body itself affects the surrounding water. The body can scatter incoming waves, generate waves through its own motion, and accelerate the fluid around it. Together, these fluid–body interactions determine how the body responds to waves.

In linear hydrodynamic analysis, these interactions are commonly described through quantities such as added mass, radiation damping, and wave excitation forces. Added mass and radiation damping are associated with waves generated by the motion of the body, while excitation forces arise from the interaction between the body and incoming waves, including both incident-wave and diffraction effects. These quantities generally depend on wave frequency, body geometry, and the mode of motion being considered.

For marine energy systems, understanding these hydrodynamic properties is essential for predicting how a device will move and respond in the ocean. They provide the connection between the geometry of the device and its dynamic response to waves.

# From Geometry to Hydrodynamics

In the previous section, we developed techniques for creating computational meshes that describe the surfaces of floating bodies. We will now use those meshes to investigate their hydrodynamic behavior.

We will begin with Meshmagick to inspect and manipulate our meshes and evaluate basic hydrostatic properties. We will then use Capytaine, a linear potential-flow boundary element method (BEM) solver, to calculate the hydrodynamic quantities needed to characterize the interaction between our bodies and the surrounding fluid.

The examples will build progressively in complexity, beginning with a simple spar buoy, followed by the addition of a heave plate, and finally a two-body system. Along the way, we will examine how changes in geometry and body configuration influence the resulting hydrodynamics.

The ultimate goal of this section is to develop the hydrodynamic inputs needed for a time-domain simulation of a wave energy converter. The hydrodynamic properties calculated with Capytaine—including added mass, radiation damping, and wave excitation forces—can be exported for use in WEC-Sim, where they are combined with the body's mass properties, restoring forces, power take-off system, and other model components to simulate the dynamic response of the complete device.
